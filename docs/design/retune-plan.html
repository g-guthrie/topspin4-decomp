Top Spin 4 Retune Plan

Revision 2. Keep the game people love. Make every situation on court pay or cost what it does in real tennis, with no single winning style.

What this is. A design to react to, built on what we have read from the game's code and data. Retail figures are measured from that data. Proposed figures are targets; those marked provisional come from a simulation that is still being re-run. Nothing has been tested on the running game yet.

Decisions made so far

  • Averages stay about where they are. The change is at the edges of the bell curve.
  • 110 mph is the fastest forehand. A hard ceiling, reached almost never.
  • Serves have no ceiling near 120. A specialist reaches about 150, rarely and at a price.
  • Six set-up grades, with a new top grade, Tee-off, for a true sitter.
  • The easier the ball, the easier Perfect. The opponent's timing grade also changes your window directly.
  • Overcharge: a full meter held while set and waiting unlocks the biggest swing.
  • Movement stays as it is, except a short list of rules that refuse real decisions. The first step from rest is left alone for now and tried last, on its own.
  • Animations are not edited. Tuning changes values, rules and which existing clip is chosen. No clip is stretched, sped up or mirrored.
  • The lock on the feet is shortened after volleys and approach shots, so the net feels dynamic. Groundstroke recovery stays as retail.

How the game works today

  1. Hold time sets power. Soft under 12 frames of hold, Medium from 12, Powerful from 53, full at 75.
  2. Timing is the gap between your release and the ball's arrival. Perfect is 20 frames before it arrives.
  3. Data graphs turn ratings, timing, position, fatigue and skills into an error chance and an aim score.
  4. The aim score picks one of four target boxes. The ball goes to the edge of that box in the stick direction.
  5. In, out, net or let is rolled before the ball leaves the racket.
  6. The flight is solved backwards to land on the chosen spot. Physics never decides whether a ball is in.

Spin and pace change how a ball looks and how it reaches the opponent. Safety and risk live in the graphs. Realism needs both halves changed together.

1. Set-up grades code data

The game has power and accuracy tables with five grade rows. Only Good is used. Drive the row from the situation, and add one row on top.

A true sitter: slow, mid-height, watched all the way, no steps neededTee-off (new)A winner-calibre ball. The only grade where the pace ceiling is reachable; wide timing window
Feet set, lots of time, ball between knee and shoulderVeryGoodFull pace, full spin, tightest aim
Normal rally ball, small adjustment stepsGoodToday's shot
Hitting on the move, or ball a little high or lowNormalLess pace, wider aim
Full run, or ball at the feet or above the shoulderBadA defensive ball
Stretched, lunging, lateVeryBadJust getting it back

Two questions, kept separate. The grade answers "how good was the situation?". Timing answers "how well did you execute?". The grade picks a row; timing picks where in that row you land. A Perfect on a Bad ball is the best Bad ball. A late swing on a Tee-off ball is the worst Tee-off ball.

Each kind of shot is judged on its own terms

So the reason to hit flat is the top of the grade table, and the reason to hit slice is the bottom of it.

  • Inputs already exist. The engine computes frames to the hit point, distance to it, the run speed needed, contact height, and whether the ball is slow and easy to reach. That last test, used today to pick the automatic swing, is a ready-made sitter detector.

What makes a Tee-off ball

All of these at once. Miss one and the grade is VeryGood at best.

ConditionProposed test
The ball is slowWell under rally pace when it reaches you
It is in the strike zoneContact between waist and shoulder
You have had timeAbout a second or more since the opponent hit it. The game's own "slow, easy ball" test uses 60 frames
Your feet are setWithin about a metre of the hit point and arrived a third of a second before contact
You are not stretched or backing upNo reach swing, not moving away from the net at contact
You are not deep behind the baselineContact no more than about a metre behind it
  • What produces one: an opponent's badly mistimed or framed ball; a defensive shot hit from a Bad or VeryBad grade; a floated slice; a short lob; a poor drop shot you reached early; a soft ball at the net.
  • What does not: an ordinary rally ball you happen to be standing still for. That is VeryGood. Tee-off needs the ball itself to be weak.
  • The moonball. Height is measured where you strike the ball, not at the top of its bounce. A high, slow ball you have time and room to let drop is struck in the strike zone and costs nothing for height. If it lands short, it is a Tee-off ball. If it lands deep and you take it from well behind the baseline, it is VeryGood: you can load a full charge or an overcharge on it, but it is not a sitter. Height only costs you when you are forced, or choose, to take the ball high: pinned by a heavy kick, or stepping in to take it early.
  • How it can still be missed: a mistimed swing, since the window is wide but not endless; an overcharge released badly; or a power swing aimed early at the lines, where the out chance is real. Hit at medium power with decent timing, it is close to a certain winner or forced error.
  • How often: a few per set between equal players, more against someone who is being outplayed. If it shows up every other rally, the conditions are too loose.
  • What it means for ratings. Today the put-away fixes the power factor at one value for everyone, so it flattens players. In the plan, Tee-off lifts each player toward their own ceiling. Power sets how fast that is: about 95 mph for an ordinary pro, 110 for the pace build. Forehand or Backhand sets how close to the lines it goes, and Volley does the same at the net. A weak wing still gets a good ball, not a great one.

The rule: the easier the ball, the easier Perfect. The window becomes a function of the ball in front of you.

  • Your opponent's quality reaches you two ways. Through the ball, which is faster, deeper and heavier when they hit it well. And directly: their timing grade scales your window. It is its own input, separate from anything about the ball, and it scales both your Perfect and your Good window.
  • Starting sizes: opponent hit Perfect, 15% narrower. Good, 7% narrower. Average, no change. Poor, 15% wider. Badly mistimed, 30% wider. This applies even when their bad shot stayed in by luck.
  • One scale. Your window is today's window multiplied by four factors: your rating on that stroke, the opponent's grade, your own set-up grade, and reaction time. The result is kept between 60% and 175% of today's.

  • It fits the engine. The game already blends a "worst" and a "best" timing curve; in retail the two are identical. The plan makes them a narrow and a wide curve and drives the blend with this scale.
  • What timing buys: Perfect lands at the top of your grade's pace and aim range with almost no short pull. Good lands in the middle. Outside the Good window you get the bottom of the range and the retail 2 to 3 m short pull.
  • The direct effect is capped, so a rally of Perfects is rising pressure and not a snowball. One good defensive shot resets it.
  • From the baseline against a good ball, Perfect is harder than today and pays more.
  • The timing rating gets real curves.

Reaction time is a threshold, not a slope. A 70 mph ball plays like a 60. It bites on volleys against a clean pass, returns of a 140 mph serve, and a ball driven at a player caught in no-man's-land.

3. The power swing and the overcharge code data

What the charge does today, for a flat forehand at perfect timing:

HoldLowest ratingsMedianTop ratings
Soft, 0 to 11 frames110.5 km/h112.5122.6
Medium, 12 to 52121.0125.1131.4
Powerful, 53124.3127.1138.9
Full, 75 and over127.1130.1145.2
  • To reach Powerful you must press at least 65 frames before contact. A re-press resets the count. Holding past 75 does nothing.
  • A powerful shot costs up to about an eighth of the stamina bar. A late full charge carries a 22% error chance.
  • So holding is expensive and barely rewarded, and ratings matter little: the weakest player's full charge beats the strongest player's soft shot by 4.5 km/h.

The change:

  • The hold has a real payoff, scaled by grade. On a Bad grade a power swing gains little and risks a lot. On VeryGood it is a different shot.
  • A power swing is a commitment. If you are still holding when the game commits the swing and the charge never reached Powerful, you asked for more than the ball allowed. That is a rushed swing: one grade point worse, on top of being late. Releasing early for a smaller swing is never rushed.
  • Overcharge. Hold a moment past a full meter without moving (10 frames, about a sixth of a second) and it enters a second state and glows. Only a slow ball gives enough time. It can never happen on a return or in a fast rally. In simulation it comes up about once in 11 matches.
  • What overcharge gives and costs: about 4% more pace than the grade's cap; a timing window 20% narrower; a mistimed one goes long; one and a half times the stamina.
  • No new button. Risk is the shot you pick and how long you hold.

Provisional targets from the flight model. Retail today: flat 0.5 to 0.75 m, topspin 0.6 to 1.4 m. The one sourced real figure is Nadal at about 0.78 m.

  • The bounce slows every ball. Speed kept: topspin 0.84, flat about 0.79, slice 0.72. Retail goes up to 1.15.
  • Topspin kicks. A heavy ball above the shoulder drops the receiver's grade unless they take it early, which needs tighter timing, or back up, which gives up court.
  • Slice stays low and slows. A low ball is hard to attack and hard to topspin.
  • Flat goes through the court. Fastest to arrive, smallest margin.
  • Spin buys margin in the error graphs, because the flight cannot.
  • Spin scales with the player. The Forehand and Backhand ratings set how much spin is available, and so how sharp an angle.
  • A better player has more pace and more spin. Only at the top of pace does the ball have to be flat and low.
  • Surfaces differ. Clay slowest and highest, grass fastest and lowest. This needs code at the bounce hook, which the retail data never uses.

Why not replace the flight with a full physics simulation? Because the game's whole shot system depends on knowing where the ball will land before it is hit: the AI, the movement planner and the animation picker all read that prediction. The plan keeps that design and makes the arcs, the bounce and the risk behave like the real thing.

The consistency check for the pace ceiling. At 110 mph a flat forehand reaches the far baseline in about half a second and must clear the net by 10 to 30 cm to land in. That is a launch angle of only 2 to 4 degrees. The same ball with heavy topspin could clear higher, but a player cannot produce both maximum pace and maximum spin, so the ceiling is always a flat, low ball.

6. Aim, margin, depth and touch code data

How aiming works today

  • The stick gives a direction only. Any push past the dead zone counts as full; no push aims at the middle of the court, 7.2 m past the net.
  • Each shot type has nested target boxes: a small one in the middle, a mid one, one on the court lines, and one 3 m outside for misses.
  • An aim score from 0 to 1 picks how far out the box is. The ball goes to the edge of that box in your stick direction.
Aim scoreStick straight upStick to the sideStick to the corner
1.0On the baselineOn the sidelineIn the corner
0.81.1 m inside the baseline0.8 m inside the sideline1.1 m and 0.7 m inside
0.52.9 m short of the baseline2.0 m inside the sideline2.9 m and 1.8 m inside
0The middle of the court, whatever the stick says
  • Where the score comes from today: almost entirely timing and shot type. At perfect timing a topspin drive scores 0.80, a flat drive 0.76, a flat power shot 0.60 and a drop shot 0.40. Mistimed, they fall to 0.4 and then 0.2. A random roll then moves it up or down.
  • The player's rating barely counts: a weak stroke rating costs only 0.03 to 0.05.
  • Then a random pull short. Even at perfect timing an in-ball lands anywhere from 0 to 3.1 m shorter than aimed. Mistimed, the pull is a fixed 2 to 3 m.

What changes, and what does not

  • The system stays. Direction-only stick, boxes, and a score that pulls poor shots to the middle. Pushing to the rim is never penalised. The earlier ideas of a stick-push risk dial and an aim that settles with hold time are both dropped; the retail score already does that job.
  • The score gets the new inputs. The set-up grade feeds it, so a Tee-off or VeryGood ball can reach the lines and a Bad one cannot. The grade tables already carry aim ranges per grade; switching the grades on connects them.
  • Timing still drives it, measured against the new, variable window. Perfect on a hard ball is worth as much as Perfect on an easy one; it is just harder to get.
  • Ratings matter. The Forehand and Backhand ratings move the score by a real amount, not 0.03.
  • Shot type keeps its character. Topspin has the best score and the lowest out chance. Flat power aims worse and misses more as pace rises. That is where the risk in a 105 mph ball comes from.
  • Depth is earned. The random short pull scales with quality: a few tens of centimetres on a well-timed, well-set shot, and the existing 2 to 3 m on a mistimed one.
  • Depth is earned. The random short pull shrinks to zero with good timing and a good grade. A badly timed ball lands short and sits up.
  • Sharp angles are gated. The aim tables already blend by where you stand. Short angles open from inside the court or out wide, and need a high aim score and enough spin. The miss goes wide, or short and attackable.
  • The chip. A slice tapped at the soft tier with the stick pulled back: a short, low ball into no-man's-land. It uses the existing slice templates.
  • The drop shot. Quality depends on the ball you receive. Off a slow ball with good timing it is deadly. Off pace or on the run it floats, and the opponent gets a wide window and a high grade.

7. Net play and the put-away code data

  • Always allowed. No sprint button needed. A rush asked for during a swing fires on the first legal frame.
  • A good approach is deep, low and takes time away. It drops the opponent's grade, so the pass comes back slower and higher.
  • A weak approach leaves them set. The pass is fast and dipping, and the reaction rule shrinks your volley window.
  • No-man's-land. A ball met low and close to the body near the service line is a half-volley: forced up, weak, floating.
  • Volleys are graded like groundstrokes instead of having zero error. A bad one is usually a weak ball, not a miss; see the table below.

What a volley produces

The main cost of a hard volley is not a miss. It is a weak ball the opponent can attack. The plan uses the outputs the volley shots already have: pace, how far out the aim box reaches, how far short the ball is pulled, and how high it floats.

The volley you faceWhat comes off your racketWhat it does to the point
Soft or high ball above the net, time to setFast, low, placed to the corner or sharply angled; or a drop volley that diesWinner, or a ball they barely reach
Firm ball at a comfortable heightSolid and deep, but returnableYou keep the net and the advantage
Hard ball with little time, or a dipping ball below the netSlower, lands mid-court, hangs in the airThey step in with a good grade and you are exposed
Hard and low at the feet, or stretchedA pop-up or a short sitter; sometimes into the net or longThey get a Tee-off ball
  • What decides the row: height of the ball against the net, time to react, incoming pace, your timing, and the Volley rating. The volley graphs already read contact height, frames to contact and depth; pace and reaction time are the inputs being switched on.
  • The Volley rating shifts every row up. A great volleyer turns the third row into the second: the hard ball comes back deep instead of hanging.
  • Outright errors live where the player overreaches. A power volley on a low or blistering ball is where balls go long or into the net. A blocked volley on the same ball stays in but sits up.
  • A ball below net height cannot be hit hard. It has to be lifted, so its pace is capped and it lands shorter. That is the price of being caught at the service line.
  • Each volley button keeps its job. Standard: safe and deep when the ball allows. Power: the put-away on a high ball, a gamble on anything else. Drop: dies off a slow ball, pops up off a fast one.

Why not come in on every point

  • The approach decides what you face. A deep, low approach puts the opponent in a Bad grade, and their pass arrives slower and higher: rows one and two. A weak approach leaves them set, and you get rows three and four.
  • Being at the net takes time from the opponent, which narrows their window. That is the reward for getting there behind a good ball.
  • Closer to the net opens the angles and exposes the lob. Further back covers the lob and leaves balls at your feet.

All of this runs through existing shot classes, graphs and aim boxes. The only code involved is feeding pace and reaction time to the graphs, and the grade.

The put-away swing today

  • 37 clips per player, all forehands at shoulder height, each with a run-in of about 4 m. A clip can be joined 9 to 137 frames before contact.
  • Tried first on flat power shots when power is above the soft tier. Blocked while you hold sprint within 3 m either side of the centre line.
  • It fixes the power factor at 0.85 on a medium hold and 0.80 on a powerful one, and removes the aim penalty for late timing. So it is a consistency bonus, not a pace bonus.
  • Topspin and lob groundstrokes use the flat lists and can never get it.
  • If the button is held into the swing, the game uses an automatic swing list: the good one for a slow, easy ball, otherwise the one holding reach and reflex swings, which lower power and aim.

They stack only at the very top. Tee-off, an overcharge, Perfect timing and a pace build together make the 110 mph forehand. Take any one away and it is an ordinary big shot.

Fixing the missing put-away

We cannot record new motion capture, so the fix has to work with what is on the disc. In order of preference:

  1. Separate the reward from the clip. The put-away bonus becomes a property of the grade. A Tee-off ball gets the put-away power and accuracy whichever swing animation fits. This alone ends "I earned it but did not get it". code
  2. Use the clips the game already has. The disc holds about 840 swing clips, including power backhands and low-ball swings. Mark the best of those as put-away animations for heights and sides the 37 special clips do not cover. data
  3. Keep the 37 showpiece clips first in line whenever they fit, so the signature animation still appears on the shoulder-high forehand.
  4. Fix the rule order. Today the walk stops at the first clip that fits, so an ordinary power swing can take the shot. On a Tee-off ball the put-away set is tried first on every stroke, topspin included. data
  5. Remove the sprint corridor block. code

Rule: animations are not edited. No clip is stretched, sped up, mirrored or re-authored. The fix changes only which existing clip is chosen and what reward the shot gets.

To check before committing to this: how many existing power clips cover each height and side well enough to read as a put-away.

8. Serve and return data

  • No hard ceiling near 120. A specialist going all out reaches about 150.
  • Pace costs first-serve percentage, placement and stamina.
  • Serves get dice. In retail a human's serve fault is a fixed step with no random roll, and soft and medium serves cannot fault on timing at all. So a server who has the rhythm never misses. The plan gives every serve a fault chance that rises with pace and with aiming close to the lines, and falls with the Serve rating, timing and fresh legs. Even a perfectly timed big serve from the best server goes in about two times in three.
  • A heavy kick serve becomes a real second weapon once the bounce kicks. Big servers with high stamina get second serves well above the tour's 96 mph average.
  • The returner gets the reaction rule: a tight but fair window, widened by the Return rating. A blocked return off a huge serve comes back short, which rewards the server who follows it in.
  • Returns stop being soft-only, so a read serve can be attacked.
  • The Reflex rating enters the match.
  • Returns are not graded by pace. See the grade section: a return is Good by default.

Keeping the big server honest

In real tennis the big serve is very strong and still does not dominate, because holding serve is not winning. The server also has to break.

9. Stats and skills data

  • No free floor. A rating under 40 adds unforced errors and slower preparation, not just less upside.
  • Smooth curves, so no rating has a cheap sweet spot.
  • Ratings lean on each other. Power without Forehand sprays. Speed without Stamina fades by the second set.
  • Each rating owns something visible. Forehand and Backhand: spin and angle. Power: pace. Volley and Return: reaction. Stamina: how long the rest lasts.
  • Skills get a cost or a counter. Topspin Invasion stops building when the rhythm changes. Monster Defense fades with stamina.
  • Abuse check. Before any value ships, simulated matches between archetype builds and min-max builds look for a dominant one. In progress.

  • How it is checked. A point-by-point model plays thousands of matches between seven archetypes and three min-max builds, using the proposed curves. Any build whose results dominate sends its numbers back for adjustment.
  • The levers, in order of reach. Grades and timing windows shape every shot. Speed, clearance and bounce shape how balls arrive. Ratings and skills shape builds. Changes are made at the widest lever that fixes the problem, so a serve issue is not patched with a skill tweak.
  • What simulation cannot tell us. Whether it feels good. That needs hands on a controller, which is why every change can be switched on and off alone.

Rollout: one change at a time

Every item is a separate value or rule. Each is switched on alone, played, and kept or reverted.

  1. Data only, low risk: rating curves without the dead zone; timing-rating curves; bounce speed loss; serve speed range and its costs.
  2. Data, feel: shot speed ranges and the power payoff; net clearance by shot.
  3. Small code: switch on the five grades; feed incoming pace and reaction time to the graphs; stick push as margin; net-rush buffering.
  4. Larger code: Tee-off grade and overcharge; put-away reward and clip set; opponent timing grade on your window; per-surface bounce; depth earned by quality.
  5. Last: skill costs and counters, tuned against the build simulation. Then, separately, the first-step experiment.

Open decisions and what is unverified

  • To tune by play: how much earlier the feet are freed after volleys and approach shots. Starting point: about half of today's 38 frames.
  • Being finished now: exact grade thresholds, timing-window sizes, net-play numbers and the build abuse check. The speed simulation is being re-run with the 110 ceiling.
  • Needs the running game: how often a put-away clip fits in play; whether the fast-ball term really is inert; the pose blend length; whether input is read before or after the simulation step. 24 such checks are written up for when the native build boots.
  • Not sourced: tour net-clearance averages; real speed loss in the air.
  • Real forehand peaks: tracking data tops out near 105 mph; higher figures come from broadcast radar and news reports.
