The test for every change: does it make a real tennis consideration count that the game ignores today, using inputs and outputs the game already has? If a change needs a new button, a new rating, an edited animation or a different way of aiming or moving, it is not in this plan.

The diagnosis: what the game ignores today

Forty-six things that make a shot easier or harder in real tennis were checked against the game's code and data. Retail properly feels twelve of them.

The idea: three things decide a shot

QuestionDecided byIn retailIn the plan

They stay separate. The grade sets what is available. Timing decides how much of it you get. A perfectly timed swing on a bad ball is the best bad ball. A late swing on a sitter is the worst sitter.

1. Ball flight, bounce and spin mostly data some code

How the retail ball works

The game does not simulate the ball step by step. It decides in, out, net or let by a dice roll, picks the landing spot, and then solves one smooth arc that ends there. The movement planner, the AI and the animation picker all rely on knowing that landing spot before the ball is hit. The plan keeps that design and makes the arc, the bounce and the risk behave like the real thing.

What realistic spin means for play

What it buysWhat it costsWhat it does to the receiver

  • Spin scales with the player. The Forehand and Backhand ratings set how much spin is available. A top rating can aim higher over the net and still land it, and reaches angles a weak wing cannot.
  • Pace and spin both rise with quality. Only at the very top of pace does the ball have to be flat and low.
  • No separate "incoming spin" input. Spin reaches the receiver through where the ball bounces to and how soon it arrives. A second penalty on top would punish the same ball twice.

3. The situation is felt: grades code data

The game's shot tables already have five rows, VeryGood to VeryBad, each with its own pace and accuracy ranges. The code always reads the Good row. The plan picks the row from the situation.

  • The middle does not move. An ordinary rally ball scores 1, which is Good, and the Good row is the retail row untouched.
  • Height is where you strike the ball, not the top of its bounce. A high ball you have time and room to let drop is struck in the strike zone and costs nothing.
  • Aiming itself does not change. You point the stick as now. What changes is how close to the lines a good or bad situation lets the ball go.

Tee-off: the ball you get to crush

All of these at once: the ball is slow; contact is between waist and shoulder; you have had about a second since the opponent hit it; your feet were set a third of a second early; you are not stretched or backing up; and you are no more than about a metre behind the baseline.

  • It comes from the opponent's weak ball: a mistimed or framed shot, a defensive ball hit under pressure, a floated slice, a short lob, a poor drop shot you reached early.
  • An ordinary rally ball you are standing still for is VeryGood, not Tee-off. So is a slow ball taken from deep behind the baseline.
  • It can be missed: by mistiming, or by going for the lines at full power. At medium power with decent timing it is close to a certain winner.
  • How often: three to four per player per set in simulation.

4. Timing windows that fit the ball small code data

The window becomes today's window multiplied by four factors, kept between 60% and 175% of today's.

FactorWhat it isRange
Your rating on that strokeForehand, Backhand, Volley or Return90% at a rating of 40, 110% at 100
The opponent's timing on the ball they just hitIts own input, separate from how the ball turned out. It applies even when their bad shot stayed in by luckPerfect 85%. Good 93%. Average 100%. Poor 115%. Badly mistimed 130%
Your gradeHow the ball's own character gets in85% to 130%
Reaction timeA threshold. No effect with 0.6 s or more; it only bites near reflex limits100% down to 70% at a quarter of a second
  • The easier the ball, the easier Perfect. A sitter after the opponent's mistake gets nearly twice today's window.
  • A Perfect from the opponent costs you, twice over and on purpose: the ball is better, and your window is narrower.
  • It cannot snowball. The floor is 60% of today's window, and each shot is judged fresh. In simulation rallies get about 4% shorter and points lost to a timing error rise from about 11% to 13%.
  • The forgiving zone stays. Retail's 19-frame band with no timing error keeps its size on a normal ball and scales with the window.
  • What timing buys does not change: the game's own curves already turn better timing into more pace and accuracy within a row.

5. The power swing is a commitment code data

Today, for a flat forehand at perfect timing:

A full charge costs up to an eighth of your stamina and buys about 4% over a medium swing. The weakest player's full charge beats the strongest player's soft shot by 4.5 km/h. So holding feels compulsory and pays almost nothing.

  • The payoff moves to the grade. On a Bad ball a power swing gains about 2% and risks a lot. On a Tee-off ball it gains about 11%.
  • A rushed swing. If you are still holding when the game commits the swing and the charge never reached Powerful, you asked for more than the ball allowed: one grade point worse. Releasing early for a smaller swing is never rushed.
  • The overcharge. Hold a sixth of a second past a full meter while standing still and the meter glows. It is worth about 4% more pace, with a window 20% narrower and a miss that goes long. Only a slow ball gives enough time, so it never happens on a return or in a fast rally. About once in 11 matches in simulation.
  • No new button. Risk is the shot you pick and how long you hold.

6. Volleys and approach shots data small code

The main cost of a hard volley is not a miss. It is a weak ball the opponent can attack.

The volley you faceWhat comes off your racketWhat it does to the point
Soft or high ball above the net, time to setFast, low, into the corner or sharply angled; or a drop volley that diesWinner, or a ball they barely reach
Firm ball at a comfortable heightSolid and deep, but returnableYou keep the net and the advantage
Hard ball with little time, or dipping below the netSlower, lands mid-court, hangs in the airThey step in with a good grade
Hard and low at the feet, or stretchedA pop-up or short sitter; sometimes net or longThey get a Tee-off ball
  • What picks the row: height against the net, time to react, incoming pace, your timing and the Volley rating. The volley graphs already read height, time and depth.
  • The Volley rating shifts every row up one.
  • Outright errors come from overreaching: a power volley on a low or blistering ball.
  • A ball below net height cannot be hit hard. It has to be lifted.
  • Why not come in every point: the approach decides which row you face. A deep, low approach puts the passer in a Bad grade. A weak one leaves them set, and you get the bottom two rows.
  • The put-away becomes how the top grades look. The reward belongs to the grade, so a Tee-off ball gets it whichever existing swing fits. The 37 showpiece clips still play when they fit. No clip is edited.

In simulation, rushing the net on every point wins 12% of matches. Behind a good approach, net points are won about 60% of the time.

7. Serve and return data code for the dice

  • Serves get dice. Every serve has a fault chance that rises with pace and with aiming near the lines, and falls with the Serve rating, timing and fresh legs. At a comfortable effort the best first-serve percentage is about 73 to 75%.
  • Pace costs percentage, placement and stamina.
  • The second serve is slower, kicks once the bounce is fixed, and can be attacked.
  • Returns are not graded by pace, and are no longer forced to the softest shots. The serve's pace reaches the returner through the timing window and the Return rating.
  • The returner can stand back to buy reaction time, at the cost of angle.
  • The Reflexes rating enters the match. Today it never arrives.

What the simulation says, and what it cannot

A point-by-point model plays thousands of matches between archetype builds and min-max builds using these rules. What this plan does not change

  • Aiming. The stick stays direction-only, with the same target boxes. Pushing to the rim is never penalised. Earlier ideas for a stick-push risk dial and an aim that settles with hold time are dropped.
  • Movement. Starts, stops, reversals, sprint and recovery are as retail.
  • Animations. None is stretched, sped up, mirrored or re-authored. Only which existing clip is chosen can change.
  • The ball's "landing spot first" design.
  • Buttons and ratings. No new ones.

Open questions

  • Two functions at the centre of tier 3 and 4, the one that picks the grade row and the one that computes the timing value, do not exist as usable code yet.
  • Whether the grade is known before the swing commits has not been confirmed in the code.
  • Switching on incoming pace wakes two dormant penalties that start far too low. They must be re-keyed in the same change so they only bite near reflex limits.
  • About two dozen questions about the game's behaviour need the running game to answer.
  • Tour net-clearance averages and real speed loss in the air are not sourced.
